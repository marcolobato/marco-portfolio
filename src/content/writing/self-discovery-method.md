Somewhere there is a folder, a shoebox, or a dead hard drive holding
the first thing you ever made and were proud of. This field note is
about why going to find it is a creativity technique, not nostalgia:
I noticed the method while rebuilding my first Flash website, the
research explains why it works, and by the end you can run it
yourself in an afternoon.

![IMAGE NEEDED: cover — the prototype's corridor or a collage of the 2000 site and the 2026 rebuild](/images/writing/self-discovery-method/01-cover.png)

## The moment this is for

My first Midjourney covers looked like everyone else's. The tool
kept offering its own styles, and clicking next was easier than
deciding. That is the moment this exercise is for: when you are
stuck using AI for creativity and every generation drifts a little
further from anything that feels like yours.

We need speedbumps in the AI process; not only for critical
thinking, but to slow down and self-reflect on our own work before
the tool pulls us to produce, produce, click next. Step back,
reflect, then continue.

## Where it came from

My first Flash homepage went up in 2000, built with whatever was at
my disposal: a logo shaped by Photoshop's new plastic filter, sharpie
markers and newspaper cutouts for the collage, animation learned one
frame at a time.

Twenty-two years later I dug it up and rebuilt it with the
technology I have today. Working through the rebuild, I kept making
the same kind of trade, old material answered with new tools:

- **The cover.** Instead of accepting Midjourney's house styles, I
  went back to the 2000 logo and asked what it cared about. The
  plastic shine, the cut paper, the hand that shows. Steering the
  render with that essence gave me an image that finally felt like
  mine.
- **The look and feel.** The old site's paper texture, scanned from
  the real 2000 assets, recreated as a live 3D material instead of a
  Flash bitmap.
- **The concept.** The original site existed to get people inspired
  into their own work. That intention carried forward whole; only
  the medium changed.

Somewhere in the middle of that work I realized I was running a
repeatable loop. The experiment had turned into an exercise.

## The four moves

1. **Retrieve.** Find the real artifact, not your memory of it. The
   folder, the shoebox, the old hard drive.
2. **Observe.** Look at it the way a stranger would. What did the
   person who made this care about? Where did they spend their
   patience?
3. **Extract.** Ask why it works this way, again, until one word is
   left. Say it out loud. That word is the principle. (The move is
   [abstraction laddering](https://www.luma-institute.com/abstraction-laddering/),
   pointed at your own history instead of a design brief.)
4. **Recontextualize.** Make one small new thing that obeys the
   word, with the tools you have today. Then put it next to the
   original.

## Why I trust it

Three pieces of evidence made me comfortable recommending this to
people, not just doing it privately.

**Nostalgic reflection measurably increases creativity.** In four
experiments at the University of Southampton, people who spent a few
minutes reflecting on a nostalgic memory wrote measurably more
creative prose than a control group; the researchers traced the
effect to a temporary rise in openness to experience. They called it
[the mnemonic muse](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103115000116).

**Creative confidence is built from recognized past creative acts.**
A [meta-analysis pooling 41 studies](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/creativity-the-art-and-science/202601/how-to-build-creative-confidence)
and over 17,000 participants links creative self-efficacy to better
creative performance. Your first finished, shared, cared-about thing
is that evidence; the exercise sends you to collect it.

**The caveat that shapes the design:** feeling creative is not the
same as performing creatively, so
[the exercise must end in making](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262688152_Self-Perceptions_of_Creativity_Do_Not_Always_Reflect_Actual_Creative_Performance),
not in a mood. Every prompt in the prototype ends with an action, in
the spirit of the Stanford d.school's
[Creative Acts for Curious People](https://dschool.stanford.edu/shop/creative-acts-for-curious-people).

## Artists have always done this

John Berger argued in [Ways of Seeing](https://www.ways-of-seeing.com/)
that what an image means depends on what sits beside it. The
side-by-side pairing is the fourth move working: put your first work
next to your current work and both change.

Van Gogh copied Hiroshige's rain print to extract *flatness*. Manet
reworked Titian's Venus and changed one variable, the *gaze*.
Sottsass's Memphis furniture carries its *texture* into the chunky,
hard-shadowed UI components of today. The prototype's gallery hangs
these pairs, each captioned with the one word that traveled.

## Make your own tools

Here is the part that still delights me: the same AI that kept
pulling me toward its house styles helped me build the speedbump
that slows it down. This prototype was made in conversation with an
AI pair, from the 3D paper room built out of the 2000 scans to the
research citations behind the method. We can now make our own tools
for creativity, fitted to our own stuck moments. Mine is this one;
I am sharing it as inspiration for yours.
